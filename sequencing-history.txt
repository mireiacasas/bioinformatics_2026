A Brief History of DNA Sequencing

DNA sequencing is the process of determining the order of
nucleotides in a DNA molecule. Since DNA contains the
genetic information of all living organisms, sequencing
technologies have profoundly transformed biological and
biomedical research. Over the past fifty years, DNA
sequencing has evolved from a slow and labor-intensive
laboratory technique into a highly automated technology
capable of generating vast amounts of genomic data in a
matter of hours.

The foundations of modern sequencing were established in
the 1970s with the development of the chain-termination
method by Frederick Sanger and colleagues. Sanger
sequencing made it possible to determine DNA sequences
with unprecedented accuracy and quickly became the
standard method used in molecular biology laboratories.
For more than two decades, Sanger sequencing was the
workhorse of genomics and played a central role in many
early genome projects.

During the 1980s and 1990s, improvements in automation,
fluorescent labeling, capillary electrophoresis, and
robotic sample handling dramatically increased sequencing
capacity. These technological advances enabled ambitious
international efforts such as the Human Genome Project,
which aimed to generate a reference sequence of the
human genome. The project demonstrated both the power
and the limitations of first-generation sequencing: while
it produced a transformative scientific resource, it
required years of work, large international collaborations,
and enormous financial investments.

A major technological revolution occurred in the mid-2000s
with the emergence of next-generation sequencing (NGS).
Unlike Sanger sequencing, which processes a relatively
small number of DNA fragments at a time, NGS technologies
perform massively parallel sequencing, generating millions
or even billions of reads in a single experiment. This
increase in throughput dramatically reduced sequencing
costs while expanding the scale of biological studies.

The introduction of NGS fundamentally changed the way
researchers approached biology. Whole-genome sequencing,
RNA sequencing, metagenomics, cancer genomics, and many
other applications became accessible to a much larger
scientific community. As sequencing became cheaper and
faster, researchers shifted from studying individual genes
to analyzing complete genomes, transcriptomes, and complex
biological systems.

Among the various NGS platforms developed over the years,
Illumina became the dominant technology for both research
and clinical applications. Illumina instruments generate
large volumes of highly accurate short reads, typically
between 100 and 300 base pairs in length. Short-read
sequencing remains the foundation of many genomic studies
because it combines excellent accuracy, high throughput,
and relatively low cost.

Today, short-read sequencing continues to support a wide
range of applications. These include whole-genome
sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, RNA-seq, microbiome
analysis, population genetics, and clinical diagnostics.
Many national and international sequencing initiatives
still rely heavily on short-read technologies because
they provide reliable data at a scale that is difficult
to match.

Despite their strengths, short reads have important
limitations. Repetitive genomic regions, structural
variants, highly duplicated sequences, and complex genome
architectures can be difficult to resolve when sequencing
reads are only a few hundred bases long. As a result,
researchers increasingly turned toward technologies capable
of generating much longer DNA reads.

This need led to the rise of third-generation sequencing,
also known as long-read sequencing. Two major platforms
currently dominate this field: Pacific Biosciences
(PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT).

PacBio sequencing is based on Single Molecule Real-Time
(SMRT) technology. Recent developments have produced
PacBio HiFi reads, which combine long read lengths with
very high accuracy. These reads are particularly useful
for assembling complete genomes, identifying structural
variants, resolving complex genomic regions, and studying
genetic variation that may be difficult to detect using
short-read sequencing alone.

Oxford Nanopore Technologies uses a fundamentally
different approach. Individual DNA molecules pass through
nanometer-scale pores, and changes in electrical current
are measured to infer the DNA sequence. Nanopore
sequencing can generate extremely long reads, sometimes
extending to hundreds of thousands or even millions of
bases. The technology also offers compact portable devices
that can be deployed outside traditional laboratory
environments.

Long-read sequencing has significantly expanded the range
of biological questions that can be addressed. Researchers
can now generate highly complete genome assemblies,
analyze large structural variants, reconstruct haplotypes,
study complex transcript isoforms, and investigate
epigenetic modifications. Many genomic regions that were
once considered inaccessible can now be characterized with
much greater confidence.

Modern genomics increasingly combines short-read and
long-read sequencing in hybrid strategies. Short reads
provide cost-effective and highly accurate data, while
long reads deliver information about large-scale genome
structure. Together they offer a more comprehensive view
of biological systems than either technology alone.

Another important development is the emergence of
pangenomes. Traditionally, genomic analyses relied on a
single reference genome. However, a single reference
cannot adequately represent the genetic diversity of an
entire species. Pangenome resources integrate genomic
information from multiple individuals and populations,
providing a richer and more representative framework for
future genetic studies.

At the same time, sequencing technologies have become a
key component of precision and genomic medicine. Clinical
applications now include the diagnosis of rare diseases,
cancer characterization, pharmacogenomics, infectious
disease surveillance, prenatal testing, and the
identification of clinically relevant genetic variants.
Sequencing is increasingly moving from specialized
research laboratories into routine healthcare settings.

The rapid growth of sequencing technologies has also
created major computational challenges. Modern projects
often generate terabytes of data that must be stored,
processed, analyzed, and shared. As a result,
bioinformatics has become an essential component of
genomics. Reproducible computational workflows, data
management, FAIR principles, cloud computing, and
high-performance computing are now central aspects of
large-scale biological research.

Current trends suggest that sequencing technologies will
continue to increase in speed, accuracy, and accessibility.
Future developments are likely to involve deeper
integration of genomics with transcriptomics, proteomics,
epigenomics, single-cell technologies, artificial
intelligence, and advanced computational methods. The
combination of improved sequencing technologies and modern
bioinformatics is creating new opportunities to understand
biological systems and to translate genomic discoveries
into medical and societal benefits.

In summary, DNA sequencing has evolved from a specialized
laboratory technique into a core technology of modern
science. While short-read sequencing remains the backbone
of many genomic applications, long-read sequencing,
pangenome resources, and genomic medicine are shaping the
current frontier of the field. Today, progress in biology
depends not only on generating data but also on the
ability to manage, analyze, interpret, and share that
information effectively.
